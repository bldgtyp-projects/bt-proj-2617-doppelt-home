title: "Window Thermal Comfort"
dek: "Connect window package assumptions to occupant comfort near large glazed areas."
---

Glazing affects both energy and occupant comfort, and both have to be assessed on a high-performance building. A
building going for any of the PHI or Phius certifications has to quantify the comfort impact under a stringent
evaluation. That evaluation looks at overall comfort from air temperature and relative humidity, and at the localized
discomfort caused by cold surfaces and drafts at the glazing.

Localized discomfort from radiant temperature asymmetry, a difference in the temperature of the surfaces around the
body, decides whether perimeter heating can be eliminated. Where the asymmetry exceeds a certain level, a compensating
heat source is needed to offset it, normally a radiator under the window.

Thermal comfort standards put the limit at a difference below 7.6°F [4.2°K] between the surfaces around a person's
body. Windows matter most here, because they run the coldest surface temperatures in the building during winter.

For <Var k="narrative.energy_code.zone" />'s winter comfort-design weather conditions [ <Var k="narrative.climate.ashrae_winter_design_temp_F"/>°F (<Var k="narrative.climate.ashrae_winter_design_temp_C"/>°C) ], Passive House certification guidelines for
this climate recommend windows with an installed R-value above <Var k="narrative.windows.comfort_target_r_value" /> hr-ft2-F/Btu [ U-value
below <Var k="narrative.windows.comfort_target_u_value" /> Btu/hr-ft2-F ]. Those produce surface temperatures PHI finds acceptable for thermal
comfort. For smaller windows with a lower 'view-factor', less visible to the occupants, slightly lower R-values may still hit the comfort target.

The recommended variant is modeled at an installed U-value of <Var k="narrative.windows.ph_window_u_value" />
Btu/hr-ft<sup>2</sup>-F [ R-<Var k="narrative.windows.ph_window_r_value" /> ], which clears the comfort threshold.
Perimeter heating can come out on that basis. Hold that number through design development, because it is an installed
value and the margin over the threshold is thin. On this building it matters most at the rear extension and the
penthouse, where the glazing runs close to full height and people sit right beside it. The small punched openings on
the front elevation have a much lower view factor and are less sensitive.

<div class="btwr-callout btwr-callout--critical">
  <strong>Installed U-value, not glass U-value</strong>
  <span>
    Installed U-value includes the frame and the installation psi-value at the rough opening. On an interior-insulated
    masonry wall the window sits in a deep, cold reveal, and a good unit installed badly loses most of its advantage.
    Draw the reveal insulation, the frame overlap detail, and the air-seal line at the perimeter. Do not leave them to
    the installer.
  </span>
</div>
