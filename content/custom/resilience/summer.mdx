The **Phius REVIVE** protocol uses the [Heat Index](https://bigladdersoftware.com/epx/docs/22-2/engineering-reference/resilience-metrics.html#heat-index) temperature to judge perceived thermal
habitability during the test-period. The results below plot the Heat Index for each zone across the full summer week.
Compliance requires that every zone demonstrate both of the following:

- 0 hours of **Heat Index** in the 'Danger' band ( > 39.4 °C [103 °F] ) or the 'Extreme Danger' band ( > 51.7 °C [125 °F] )
- 0 **'deadly days'**, where the daily mean zone temperature exceeds the Mora survivability threshold for that day's mean humidity

  <strong>Result: does not comply</strong>
  <span>
    The house clears the 'Extreme Danger' threshold with room to spare and then fails on the 'Danger' band. Peak Heat
    Index reaches **46.8 °C [116.2 °F]** at the garden level and stays below 45.5 °C [113.8 °F] in the coolest zone,
    so every zone records **0 hours in 'Extreme Danger'**, a margin of 4.9 °C below that threshold. Against an
    allowance of zero, however, each zone accumulates **41 to 42 hours in 'Danger'**, plus 37 to 39 hours in 'Extreme
    Caution' and 47 to 50 hours in 'Caution'. Every zone also records **3 'deadly days'**, on July 8, 9 and 10,
    against an allowance of zero.
  </span>

#### The failure is heat accumulation, not a single peak hour

This is what determines the response, and it is worth following carefully because it points away from the obvious
remedies.

Interior dry-bulb temperature peaks at only **31.6 °C [88.9 °F]**, and interior relative humidity runs between 90%
and 100%, touching saturation on several nights. Neither number is extreme on its own. What produces the result is
that they persist. From July 6 through July 10 the outdoor temperature never falls below 29.0 °C [84.2 °F], so the
window-opening control, which requires the interior to be at least 1.5 °C warmer than outside, **never triggers for
five consecutive days**. Over those five days the house climbs steadily from 23.3 °C to 30.3 °C with no mechanism to
shed heat other than 0.07 ACH of infiltration.

The ventilative cooling itself works. When outdoor air finally becomes available late on July 10, the windows open at
6 to 20 ACH and the Heat Index falls from 44 °C to 26 °C in five hours. The strategy is sound. The heat wave simply
does not offer it an opening.

#### Where the heat actually comes from

Over the 47-hour window that produces the 'Danger' hours, heat delivered to the occupied zones divides as follows
(shares of the net gain, so the two negative paths are heat leaving the building):

| Path | Share |
| --- | --- |
| Windows, solar and conduction combined | 60% |
| Occupants | 55% |
| Exterior walls | 9% |
| Roof | -6% |
| Infiltration | -19% |

The masonry walls are not the problem. Over that window the brick absorbed 176 kWh and delivered only 9 kWh to the
interior, because the insulation sits inboard of the mass. The wall is doing exactly what a heavy insulated masonry
assembly should do. The roof and infiltration are net **losses**. Glazing and occupants carry the load, and occupant
gain is fixed by the protocol.

#### What would actually change the result

1. **Specify the interior blinds tightly.** Interior shading is worth far more than it is usually given credit for
   here, and the performance swing between products is large. A dark or open-weave fabric passing 80% of incident
   solar removes under 10% of the gain and is close to no shade at all. A white roller or venetian blind at 15% to
   30% solar transmittance and 55% to 70% reflectance is three to four times more effective. This is a
   specification decision rather than a construction cost, which makes it the cheapest available move.
2. **Add exterior shading, which the standard expects anyway.** REVIVE §6.4.3(a) prescribes **exterior** blinds
   that close above 100 W/m<sup>2</sup> of incident radiation. Exterior devices stop solar before it crosses the
   glass, so they outperform any interior product and they are the strongest passive measure available on this
   building. A rooftop canopy over the penthouse, fixed or operable louvers at the fourth-floor terrace glazing, and
   deciduous planting at the rear are all worth carrying into the next phase. See
   [Summer Radiation](/windows/#summer-radiation).
3. **Hold the glazing SHGC down, and lower it further at the exposed glass.** The package carries
   <Var k="narrative.user_defined.rec_window_shgc" /> SHGC throughout. Specifying a lower SHGC at the penthouse and
   the fourth-floor terrace glazing, where the exposure is worst, cuts the largest single gain path directly. It
   costs some winter solar gain at those openings, and the summer case governs there.
4. **Backup power for cooling, which the protocol explicitly contemplates.** REVIVE §6.4.3(b) allows a compliance
   path in which a heat pump runs on on-site renewable power, falling back to a 27 °C [80.6 °F] setback when no
   renewable power is available. Paired with the penthouse-roof PV array already shown on the drawing set and a
   modest battery, this is the one measure that closes the gap with certainty rather than by degrees. Given how much
   of the residual load is occupant heat and humidity that no envelope measure can remove, we would carry this
   forward as the primary recommendation.
5. **Night flushing is already working and does not need enlarging.** The house vents at up to 20 ACH the moment
   outdoor conditions permit. Adding operable area will not help, because the binding constraint is the number of
   hours when outdoor air is cool enough to use, not the size of the opening.
