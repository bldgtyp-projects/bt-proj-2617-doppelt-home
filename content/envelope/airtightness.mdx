The primary role of airtightness in buildings is to avoid interstitial condensation and mold/moisture damage to the
structure during the winter and shoulder-season months. In hot climates, airtightness also restricts warm outdoor air
and moisture vapor ingress from the exterior. That reduces energy consumption for cooling and dehumidification while
improving occupant comfort and building resiliency.

Airtightness also has a simple linear relationship to the building's heat loss. The more airtight the construction,
the less heat is lost in winter and the better the energy performance. Airtightness has a large effect on indoor
relative humidity during the summer months too, with a corresponding drop in cooling energy consumption and
dehumidification need.

Code-minimum construction in most states requires an airtightness rate of somewhere between 3 to 7 air-changes per
hour (ACH), and the <VarLink hrefKey="narrative.energy_code.code_airtightness_url" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><Var k="narrative.energy_code.name" /> / <Var k="narrative.energy_code.zone" /></VarLink>
requires a continuous air barrier across the whole thermal envelope, documented and tested. The model's code-minimum
variant assumes 3.0 ACH50 as a representative baseline for construction of this type.

To meet the recommended building performance level, this project should target an airtightness level better than
<strong><Var k="narrative.certification.ph_ach_limit" /> ACH@50Pa</strong>. Improving airtightness to this low level
is one of the best ways to improve performance, increase comfort and durability, and reduce the risk of uncontrolled
wintertime air leakage.

#### Why this building in particular

A masonry row house leaks in specific, predictable places. Almost all of them are concealed by the time anyone thinks
to test:

- **Embedded floor joist ends.** Every joist pocket in the front, rear and party walls is a direct path from the
  interior to the masonry cavity. There are several hundred of them across six levels.
- **The party wall.** Shared masonry is rarely solid, and it connects to the neighbors' cavities, chases and flues.
- **Floor-line transitions.** The air barrier has to turn from the wall, across the joist bay, and back to the wall at
  every level, with the joists in the way.
- **The stair core and the new penthouse.** A full-height stair drives a strong winter stack effect, so leaks at the
  top of the building get pulled hardest.
- **Existing flues, chases and abandoned penetrations,** which normally turn up during demolition rather than during
  design.

#### How to hit the target

1. Mark the continuous air-barrier line on the architectural sections and plans, and issue a schedule of every
   transition and penetration that line has to survive. That drawing is scheduled as part of the envelope design
   phase.
2. Write <Var k="narrative.certification.ph_ach_limit" /> ACH50 into the construction documents as a contractual
   performance requirement.
3. Test early. Run a blower-door test as soon as the air barrier is continuous, before insulation, finishes or the
   service cavity go in. A failing test at that point is a morning's repair. The same test at substantial completion
   is a demolition job.
4. Test again at completion for verification.
5. Budget for a second round of sealing. On a retrofit of this age, plan for it.
