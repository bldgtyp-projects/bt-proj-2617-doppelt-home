The envelope recommendations above were developed with code compliance in mind, so that one set of assemblies serves
both the performance goals and the filing. This section summarizes where the project stands. The compliance work
itself, including the tabular envelope analysis and the filing coordination, is a later phase of our scope.

#### The building falls under the Commercial Provisions

The <VarLink hrefKey="narrative.energy_code.link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><Var k="narrative.energy_code.name" /></VarLink>
defines a Residential building ([C202][c202]) to include townhouses having not more than three stories above grade
plane, and a Commercial building as everything else. At
<Var k="narrative.user_defined.stories_above_grade" /> stories above grade plane, this house is a Commercial building
for energy-code purposes, whatever its single-family use and R-3 occupancy classification say. The existing building
already exceeded that threshold, so the new penthouse is not what causes it.

The practical consequences are a cap on vertical fenestration area ([C402.5.1][c402.5.1]), assembly U-factor
requirements in place of the residential R-value table ([C402.1.2][c402.1.2]), and the electrical power and
lighting provisions of [C405][c405].

The lighting exposure is smaller than the Commercial label suggests. [C405.1][c405.1] exempts dwelling units that
comply with C405.2.10, C405.3.3 and C405.6. Of those three, [C405.2.10][c405.2.10] reaches only hotels, motels,
vacation timeshares and congregate living facilities, and [C405.6.1][c405.6.1] meters dwelling units in Group R-2
buildings. Neither describes this house. What is left is [C405.3.3][c405.3.3], which asks for lamps rated at 65
lumens per watt or luminaires at 45 lumens per watt. There is no watts-per-square-foot lighting power budget to meet
and no commercial control package to design. Pass the efficacy floor to the lighting designer and the C405 obligation
is done.

#### The work is an alteration, so Chapter C5 governs

[C401.2.1][c401.2.1] sends commercial buildings to Sections C402 through C406 and C408, with an exception for
alterations and additions to existing buildings, which comply with [Chapter C5][chapter-c5] instead. This project is an
alteration under [C503][c503] plus an addition at the penthouse under [C502][c502]. Chapter C5 then
routes the new and altered assemblies back to the Chapter C4 requirements, so the targets in the table above still
apply. Two consequences are worth carrying into the compliance phase.

[C503.2][c503.2] lets an area-weighted average U-factor across the new and altered envelope satisfy the U-factor
requirements of Section C402.1.4, and it bars any reduction in existing insulation R-value. [C503.2.4][c503.2.4]
sets the trigger that matters here: where exterior wall coverings and fenestration are replaced across a full elevation, insulation must be
brought to the Table C402.1.3 continuous-insulation value, to Table C402.1.2 compliance, or to an approved design.

#### The fenestration ratio clears the cap, which settles the compliance path

[C402.5.1][c402.5.1] caps vertical fenestration at 30% of gross above-grade wall area, excluding opaque doors
and opaque spandrel panels, and caps skylights at 3% of gross roof area. Glazing is about
<Var k="narrative.user_defined.window_to_envelope_ratio" />% of the modeled thermal envelope area, and no skylights
are planned. Measured against the above-grade wall area the code actually counts, and excluding the party wall against
the conditioned neighbor, the ratio sits well below the cap. The relief in
[C402.5.1.1][c402.5.1.1], which buys 40% in exchange for daylight responsive controls, is not needed.

That result matters more than it looks. The component performance method in [C402.1.4][c402.1.4] is an
alternative to both the prescriptive U-, F-, psi-, chi- and C-factor tables and the fenestration area limits of
C402.5.1. Its Equation 4-1 carries penalty terms for excess vertical fenestration (VF) and excess skylight area (VS),
each computed from the area by which the proposal exceeds the cap. Both go to zero here, so the equation reduces to a
straightforward area-weighted trade-off. [C401.2.2][c401.2.2] offers 2025 NYC ASHRAE 90.1 as the other route,
and clearing the cap is what keeps the project off it.

#### The thermal-bridge work counts twice

The 2025 code added linear and point thermal transmittance to Equation 4-1 as the TP and TT terms, built from the
psi- and chi-factors in Table C402.1.4 for the junctions listed in [C402.7][c402.7]. Where a detail beats the
"compliant" tabulated value, [C402.1.4][c402.1.4] item 3 lets the proposed psi- or chi-factor be established by
thermal analysis, testing or other approved sources. The two-dimensional simulations scheduled for this project
therefore serve as Passive House documentation and as code credit from the same run.
[C402.7.1][c402.7.1] names the junctions to detail, and the intermediate floor to wall intersection and the roof
edge and parapet are both on that list.

One detail matters for an interior-insulated masonry building. Equation 4-1 assigns a value of zero to the psi- and
chi-values of thermal bridges caused by materials with a thermal conductivity below
<Var k="narrative.user_defined.code_tb_conductivity_limit" />, and [C402.7][c402.7] exception 4 excuses those
same bridges from the mitigation and documentation requirement. Softwood runs near 0.8 in those units, well under the
line, so the embedded wood joist ends, which are the governing moisture risk on this building, carry no code
liability. Steel, concrete and masonry elements are above the line and do count. The joist ends still drive the
hygrothermal work, which is a durability question rather than a code one.
